## 2. Content pillars ("scenarios")

1. **Crush and attraction**: "Signs someone likes you", "Things that make you attractive". The biggest traffic driver.
2. **Self-diagnosis and identity**: "Signs you're an introvert / empath / highly sensitive / INFJ". Viewers watch these to feel seen.
3. **Toxic people**: narcissists, manipulators, gaslighting, toxic friends. These play on protective curiosity.
4. **Childhood and trauma**: "Signs you were emotionally neglected", "Things people raised by strict parents do". This pillar has high emotional pull and lots of comments.
5. **Mental health awareness**: hidden depression, anxiety, burnout, loneliness. Always gentle, with a disclaimer.
6. **Quizzes and interactive videos**: "QUIZ: Are you…?", "Which ___ are you?". These drive comments and rewatches.
7. **Self-improvement**: habits, "Psychological tricks", "Things emotionally intelligent people do".

**The shared recipe:** a number, a noun that points at the viewer ("signs you…", "things you…"), and a topic that's a little vulnerable.

## 3. Hook analysis

The first 15–30 seconds almost always follow this pattern:

| Hook type | Template | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Relatable question** | "Have you ever felt like…?" / "Do you often find yourself…?" | The viewer answers "yes" in their head and is now invested |
| **Mirror statement** | "You're the one everyone comes to for advice, but who do *you* go to?" | Describes a private experience, so it feels personal |
| **Curiosity gap** | "There might be a reason you feel this way, and it goes back further than you think." | Promises an explanation for something the viewer already feels |
| **Stat or study teaser** | "According to psychologists, …" | Borrowed authority, so the video feels educational, not gossip |
| **Validation promise** | "If this sounds like you, you're not alone." | Emotional safety keeps sensitive viewers watching |

Then comes the **signature transition**:
> "Hey, Psych2Goers, welcome back to our channel…" followed by a brief **disclaimer** ("This video is for educational purposes only and isn't a substitute for professional help"), and then "With that said, let's get started." or "Let's dive in."

### How each point is built (repeat 5–9 times)
1. **Numbered header**: "Number 1: You apologize for things that aren't your fault."
2. **Scenario**: a short, concrete moment, usually in second person ("Someone bumps into *you*, and *you* say sorry.")
3. **Psychology**: a named concept, researcher or study, explained in one or two plain sentences.
4. **Validation or reframe**: "This doesn't make you weak. It's a skill you learned to stay safe."
5. Optional micro-engagement: "Sound familiar?"

### Outro pattern
- A summary that ends on an empowering reframe ("Your feelings matter too.")
- A call to comment on something specific: "Which of these did you relate to the most? Let us know in the comments below."
- A soft mental-health note: "If you're struggling, please reach out to a licensed professional."
- "If you found this video helpful, be sure to like and share it with someone who might benefit… and subscribe for more content. **Thanks for watching, and see you next time!**"
- References appear on screen or in the description (APA style).

### Titles and thumbnails
- Titles: `[Number] Signs/Things + You/Someone + [emotional payoff]`. Odd numbers (5, 7, 9) are common. Parenthetical twists work well: *"(Even if You Don't Think So!)"*.
- Thumbnails: pastel background, one or two blob characters showing an obvious emotion, and 2–4 words of bold text that add to the title rather than repeat it.
- Length: about 900–1,400 spoken words (roughly 6–9 minutes at a calm pace of ~150 wpm).

## 4. Original script: "7 Signs You Were the 'Easy Child' Growing Up"

**Pillar:** Childhood and trauma, crossed with self-identity
**Target length:** ~7 min (~1,100 words)
**Alternate titles:**
- *7 Signs You Had to Be the "Easy" Child (And How It Affects You Now)*
- *Were You the "Low-Maintenance" Kid? 7 Signs It Still Affects You*

**Thumbnail idea:** a small blob character smiling while holding a giant stack of other blobs' problems. Text: **"I'm fine :)"**

---

### [0:00] HOOK
*(Visual: a small blob sits quietly at a dinner table while two bigger blobs argue. It smiles and gives a thumbs-up.)*

**NARRATOR:**
Were you the kid who never caused any trouble? The one teachers called "so mature for your age," who did homework without being asked and somehow always knew when *not* to ask for something?

*(Visual: a gold star sticker labeled "Low-Maintenance" lands on the blob's head.)*

It might have felt like a compliment back then. But being the "easy child" often means something else: you learned early that your needs came second. And years later, that lesson can still show up in quiet ways you might not notice.

### [0:30] INTRO + DISCLAIMER
*(Visual: Psych2Go logo pop.)*

Hey, Psych2Goers, welcome back to our channel! Before we begin, please note that this video is for educational purposes only and isn't meant to diagnose anyone. Everyone's childhood is different, and relating to a few of these points doesn't mean something is "wrong" with you or your family. With that said, let's get started.

---

### [0:50] #1: YOU FEEL GUILTY ASKING FOR HELP
*(Visual: the blob stares at a phone showing a half-typed message: "Hey, could you maybe…" It deletes it.)*

Number one: asking for help feels like a burden.

Even when you're overwhelmed, you'd rather struggle alone than "bother" anyone. Maybe you type the message, reread it five times, and then delete it.

Psychologists sometimes describe this pattern as **compulsive self-reliance**, a term from attachment research. When a child's needs aren't reliably met, they may adapt by learning to need as little as possible. It worked back then. As an adult, it can leave you feeling very alone, even when you're surrounded by people who'd gladly help.

Sound familiar?

---

### [1:40] #2: YOU'RE EXTREMELY GOOD AT READING THE ROOM
*(Visual: the blob walks into a room, and small weather icons float above everyone: storm cloud, sun, drizzle.)*

Number two: you notice mood changes before anyone says a word.

A slight sigh, a door closed a little too hard, a shorter-than-usual text. You pick up on all of it. As a kid, sensing tension early may have helped you stay out of the way or smooth things over before they got worse.

This kind of **hypervigilance** can make you incredibly empathetic. But constantly scanning for other people's emotions is exhausting, and it leaves little energy left over for your own.

---

### [2:25] #3: YOU DOWNPLAY YOUR OWN PROBLEMS
*(Visual: a friend blob holds up a tiny bandage and cries. Our blob hides a huge cast behind its back and says, "Oh, I'm good!")*

Number three: your go-to answer is "I'm fine."

When someone asks how you are, you instinctively shrink your struggles. "It's not a big deal." "Other people have it worse." "I'll figure it out."

Research on **emotional suppression** (for example, work by psychologists James Gross and Oliver John) suggests that habitually hiding your feelings is linked to lower well-being and feeling less connected to others. Pushing emotions down doesn't make them disappear. It just makes them lonelier.

---

### [3:10] #4: YOU TAKE CARE OF EVERYONE ELSE'S FEELINGS
*(Visual: the blob juggles hearts labeled "Mom," "Friend," "Coworker," "Sibling." Its own heart sits on the floor, forgotten.)*

Number four: you became the family peacekeeper, and you still are.

Maybe you were the one who comforted a parent, played messenger between arguing adults, or looked after younger siblings. Family therapists call this **parentification**: a child taking on emotional or practical responsibilities meant for adults.

Today, it might look like being "the therapist friend," always listening but rarely being listened to. Caring about others is a beautiful trait. It just shouldn't have to cost you yourself.

---

### [3:55] #5: YOU STRUGGLE TO KNOW WHAT YOU ACTUALLY WANT
*(Visual: a waiter blob asks, "What would you like?" Our blob looks at the others. A thought bubble shows a question mark.)*

Number five: "I don't mind, whatever you want!"

Choosing a restaurant, a movie, even a career path. You often defer to others because, honestly, you're not sure what *you* prefer. When a child spends years adjusting to everyone else, they may not get much practice noticing their own desires.

The good news? Preferences are like muscles. You can start small: pick the song, choose the snack, say "actually, I'd like Thai food." Every small choice is a way of getting to know yourself again.

---

### [4:40] #6: PRAISE MAKES YOU UNCOMFORTABLE, BUT CRITICISM STICKS
*(Visual: a compliment bubble bounces off the blob. A tiny criticism bubble sticks to it like glue.)*

Number six: compliments slide off, but criticism stays for days.

If you were valued for being "good" and "no trouble," your sense of worth may have become tied to performance and pleasing others. So a small mistake can feel like proof that you've failed at the one job you had: being easy.

This is linked to **perfectionism** and **people-pleasing**, and to what therapist Pete Walker described as the **"fawn" response**, a way of staying safe by keeping everyone else happy.

---

### [5:25] #7: YOU FEEL UNSEEN, EVEN IN CLOSE RELATIONSHIPS
*(Visual: the blob stands in a group photo, slightly faded compared to everyone else.)*

And number seven: deep down, you wonder if anyone really knows you.

Because you've gotten so good at being low-maintenance, people may not realize you have needs at all. And while being "the strong one" can earn respect, it can also leave a quiet ache: *Does anyone see me when I'm not being helpful?*

If that question hit home, please know this: you deserve to be cared for, not just because of what you do for others, but simply because you exist.

---

### [6:05] OUTRO
*(Visual: the gold "Low-Maintenance" sticker peels off the blob's head. A friend blob hands it a cup of tea. The blob, surprised, accepts it and smiles for real.)*

Being the "easy child" often means you grew up adapting, anticipating, and holding things together. Those are real strengths. But you don't have to be easy to be worthy of love. You're allowed to take up space, to ask for help, and to have needs that aren't convenient.

So, which of these signs did you relate to the most? Let us know in the comments below. You never know who might feel less alone reading your story.

And if any of this brought up difficult feelings, please consider reaching out to a licensed mental health professional. You don't have to work through it on your own.

If you found this video helpful, be sure to give it a like and share it with someone who might need to hear this today. Don't forget to subscribe for more content like this. Thanks for watching, and remember: your needs matter too. See you next time!

*(End card: two suggested videos, e.g. "Signs of Emotional Neglect" and "Why You Always Feel Like a Burden".)*
